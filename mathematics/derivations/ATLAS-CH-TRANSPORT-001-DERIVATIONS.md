# ATLAS-CH-TRANSPORT-001 — Derivation Packet

## Scope

This packet establishes the exact finite-dimensional claims used by **Representation as Transport**.

It does not prove that arbitrary learned networks are exact differential flows, geodesics, parallel transports, vector transports, or optimal-transport maps.

## D1. Finite representation transport

Let

\[
z_{k+1}=\Phi_k(z_k).
\]

Then recursively

\[
z_K
=
(\Phi_{K-1}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi_0)(z_0).
\]

This identity requires no continuous-time interpretation.

If

\[
\Phi_k(z)=z+F_k(z),
\]

the identity contribution is exact at the discrete-map level.

Nothing here implies that \(F_k\) is generated by one autonomous vector field.

## D2. Step-scaled residual stage

For

\[
\Phi_{k,h}(z)=z+hF_k(z),
\]

one may compare the update to forward Euler when a declared ODE reference exists.

Without that reference, \(h\) is only an explicit residual scale.

The distinction is:

\[
\text{same algebraic form}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{same mathematical semantics}.
\]

## D3. Sphere tangent condition

For

\[
S^{n-1}=\{u:\|u\|_2=1\},
\]

the tangent space at \(u\) is

\[
T_uS^{n-1}
=
\{\xi:u^\top\xi=0\}.
\]

Hence tangent membership depends on the base point.

## D4. Normalized sphere retraction

For \(u\in S^{n-1}\) and tangent \(\xi\) such that \(u+\xi\neq0\),

\[
R_u(\xi)=\frac{u+\xi}{\|u+\xi\|_2}.
\]

By construction,

\[
\|R_u(\xi)\|_2=1.
\]

Therefore the endpoint is feasible.

This proves only constraint preservation.

## D5. Retraction versus exponential map

Let

\[
u=(1,0),
\qquad
\xi=(0,1).
\]

Since \(u^\top\xi=0\), \(\xi\in T_uS^1\).

The normalized retraction is

\[
R_u(\xi)
=
\frac{(1,1)}{\sqrt2}
=
(\cos(\pi/4),\sin(\pi/4)).
\]

For the unit sphere with the standard metric, the exponential map along a unit tangent \(\xi\) at unit time is

\[
\operatorname{Exp}_u(\xi)
=
\cos(1)u+\sin(1)\xi
=
(\cos1,\sin1).
\]

Because

\[
\pi/4\neq1,
\]

the endpoints differ.

Thus

\[
R_u(\xi)\neq \operatorname{Exp}_u(\xi).
\]

The retraction is feasible but not the exact geodesic endpoint for this step.

## D6. Tangent data is base-point-specific

Again take

\[
u_0=(1,0),
\qquad
v_0=(0,1).
\]

Then

\[
u_0^\top v_0=0.
\]

Move the state to

\[
u_1
=
R_{u_0}(v_0)
=
\frac{(1,1)}{\sqrt2}.
\]

Now

\[
u_1^\top v_0
=
\frac1{\sqrt2}.
\]

Therefore

\[
v_0\notin T_{u_1}S^1.
\]

The orthogonal tangent projector at \(u_1\) is

\[
P_{u_1}=I-u_1u_1^\top.
\]

Applying it,

\[
P_{u_1}v_0
=
v_0-(u_1^\top v_0)u_1
=
(0,1)
-\frac1{\sqrt2}\frac{(1,1)}{\sqrt2}
=
\left(-\frac12,\frac12\right).
\]

This is a valid tangent vector because

\[
u_1^\top P_{u_1}v_0=0.
\]

Projection is one possible local operation. It is not automatically parallel transport.

## D7. Order-sensitive constrained transport witness

Take

\[
u_0=(1,0,0),
\quad
a=(0,1,0),
\quad
b=(0,0,1).
\]

At the initial point,

\[
u_0^\top a=u_0^\top b=0.
\]

### A then B

First,

\[
u_a
=
R_{u_0}(a)
=
\frac{(1,1,0)}{\sqrt2}.
\]

Since

\[
u_a^\top b=0,
\]

the second increment is tangent at the intermediate point.

Then

\[
u_{ab}
=
R_{u_a}(b).
\]

Before normalization,

\[
u_a+b
=
\left(\frac1{\sqrt2},\frac1{\sqrt2},1\right).
\]

Its squared norm is

\[
\frac12+\frac12+1=2.
\]

Hence

\[
u_{ab}
=
\left(\frac12,\frac12,\frac1{\sqrt2}\right).
\]

### B then A

Similarly,

\[
u_b
=
R_{u_0}(b)
=
\frac{(1,0,1)}{\sqrt2},
\]

and

\[
u_b^\top a=0.
\]

Therefore

\[
u_{ba}
=
R_{u_b}(a)
=
\left(\frac12,\frac1{\sqrt2},\frac12\right).
\]

The endpoints differ because the second and third coordinates differ:

\[
u_{ab}-u_{ba}
=
\left(
0,
\frac12-\frac1{\sqrt2},
\frac1{\sqrt2}-\frac12
\right)
\neq0.
\]

Both remain unit norm.

Their inner product is

\[
\begin{aligned}
u_{ab}^\top u_{ba}
&=
\frac14
+\frac{1}{2\sqrt2}
+\frac{1}{2\sqrt2}\\
&=
\frac14+\frac1{\sqrt2}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus the path order changes the constrained endpoint.

## D8. Constraint preservation is not information preservation

Let

\[
N(x)=\frac{x}{\|x\|},
\qquad x\neq0.
\]

For any \(c>0\),

\[
N(cx)=N(x).
\]

Therefore \(N\) is non-injective along positive rays.

Any transport pipeline containing this normalization can erase radial information unless another channel preserves it.

Hence

\[
\text{constraint preservation}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{information preservation}.
\]

## D9. Discrete composition versus ODE flow

An explicit ODE model declares

\[
\dot z=f(z,t;\theta).
\]

Under the hypotheses guaranteeing a flow, the finite-time map may be denoted

\[
\varphi_{t_1,t_0}(z_0).
\]

A learned stack instead directly supplies

\[
\Phi_{K-1}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi_0.
\]

A relation between the two requires additional assumptions or a declared numerical construction.

The finite composition alone does not identify a unique \(f\).

## D10. Autonomous versus stage-dependent transport

If

\[
\Phi_k=\Phi
\]

for every \(k\), the repeated map is stage-homogeneous.

If

\[
\Phi_k
\]

changes with depth, the system is stage-dependent.

A continuous analogy for the latter is generally nonautonomous:

\[
\dot z=f(z,t),
\]

unless stronger structure justifies another interpretation.

## D11. Differential transport versus state transport

For differentiable \(\Phi_k\), perturbations propagate locally by

\[
\delta z_{k+1}
=
J_{\Phi_k}(z_k)\delta z_k.
\]

This Jacobian action is a differential pushforward in ambient coordinates.

It is not automatically a manifold vector transport, because:

- domain and codomain tangent spaces must be specified;
- constraints may require projection;
- the map may be non-invertible;
- a chosen connection/parallel-transport rule is additional geometry.

## D12. Downstream Neural Krylov boundary

The downstream chapter may take as input a local linearized transport operator such as

\[
J_{\Phi_k}(z_k)
\]

or a composed Jacobian product when explicitly declared.

But classical Krylov guarantees require their own operator, subspace, projection, conditioning, and arithmetic hypotheses.

Therefore

\[
\text{transport interpretation}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{Krylov convergence theorem}.
\]

## Durable propositions

1. A finite neural stack is exactly a finite composition of its declared stage maps.
2. A sphere retraction preserves the sphere constraint.
3. A normalized retraction need not equal the sphere exponential map.
4. Tangent membership is base-point-specific.
5. Old tangent data may cease to be tangent after the state moves.
6. Two feasible constrained stages can produce different endpoints when order is reversed.
7. Normalization can destroy radial information.
8. Exact ODE-flow semantics require additional structure beyond residual algebra.
9. Stage-dependent maps require stage-dependent/nonautonomous semantics unless stronger structure is established.
10. Representation transport does not itself provide downstream Krylov guarantees.
