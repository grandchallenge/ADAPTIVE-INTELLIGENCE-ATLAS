Large linear problems are often attacked without solving the whole space at once.

The key move is to build a sequence of informative directions from repeated application of the same operator to a starting residual. The resulting trial space is not arbitrary. It is generated by the operator itself.

For a square operator $A$ and starting residual $r_0$,

\[
\mathcal{K}_m(A,r_0)=\operatorname{span}\{r_0,Ar_0,\ldots,A^{m-1}r_0\}.
\]

For $Ax=b$ with starting point $x_0$ and $r_0=b-Ax_0$, an iterative subspace method seeks

\[
x_m\in x_0+\mathcal{K}_m(A,r_0).
\]

This chapter develops the classical structure behind that idea.

## 1. Why operator-generated directions matter

A direct method tries to expose enough of the full matrix structure to solve the system outright.

An iterative subspace method instead asks:

- which directions become visible after repeated operator action;
- what reduced problem appears in those directions;
- whether the reduced correction meaningfully reduces the target error or residual;
- how numerical conditioning changes that interpretation.

This distinction matters when $A$ is large, sparse, structured, or available only through a routine that computes $Av$.

## 2. Arnoldi: general matrices

Starting from a normalized residual $v_1$, Arnoldi repeatedly applies $A$ and orthogonalizes the result against the basis already built.

In exact arithmetic it produces orthonormal columns $V_m$ and an upper-Hessenberg reduced relation

\[
AV_m=V_{m+1}\overline H_m.
\]

Three objects must remain distinct:

- the generated trial space;
- the orthonormal basis used to represent it;
- the reduced projected problem solved in that basis.

The Arnoldi relation is general for square matrices. It does not require symmetry.

## 3. Lanczos: the symmetric specialization

When A is symmetric, or Hermitian in the complex case, the Arnoldi projection becomes symmetric as well as upper Hessenberg.

A symmetric Hessenberg matrix is tridiagonal.

The exact-arithmetic recurrence therefore collapses to three terms:

\[
\beta_{j-1}v_{j-1}+\alpha_jv_j+\beta_jv_{j+1}=Av_j.
\]

This is the Lanczos structure.

The short recurrence is a structural consequence of symmetry in exact arithmetic. It is not a promise that floating-point basis vectors remain exactly orthogonal forever.

## 4. Galerkin projection

Write

\[
x_m=x_0+V_my.
\]

Then

\[
r_m=r_0-AV_my.
\]

A Galerkin method chooses y so that the new residual is orthogonal to the trial space:

\[
V_m^{\mathsf T}r_m=0.
\]

This gives the reduced system

\[
(V_m^{\mathsf T}AV_m)y=V_m^{\mathsf T}r_0.
\]

The method therefore solves a smaller problem whose dimension is m rather than the ambient dimension.

## 5. Minimum residual is a different projection rule

A minimum-residual method instead chooses y to minimize

\[
\lVert r_0-AV_my\rVert_2.
\]

With an Arnoldi basis, this becomes a small least-squares problem involving Hbar_m.

The distinction is important:

- Galerkin imposes an orthogonality condition;
- minimum residual explicitly minimizes residual norm;
- neither statement is automatically the same as minimizing solution error in Euclidean norm.

## 6. Residual and error are related, not identical

If x* is the exact solution and

\[
e_m=x_\star-x_m,
\]

then

\[
Ae_m=r_m.
\]

For nonsingular A,

\[
e_m=A^{-1}r_m.
\]

Therefore

\[
\lVert e_m\rVert_2\le\lVert A^{-1}\rVert_2\lVert r_m\rVert_2.
\]

A small residual can support a useful error bound when the inverse operator is well behaved.

A badly conditioned operator can magnify residual-to-error conversion.

This is why convergence statements must say which norm and which quantity are being controlled.

## 7. Polynomial viewpoint

Every correction in the m-step generated subspace can be written as

\[
q_{m-1}(A)r_0
\]

for a polynomial of degree at most m-1.

The residual has the form

\[
r_m=p_m(A)r_0
\]

with $p_m(0)=1$.

This viewpoint connects iterative linear algebra to polynomial approximation.

It also explains why spectral distribution, clustering, non-normality, and the initial residual can strongly affect convergence.

## 8. Exact two-dimensional witness

Take

\[
A=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,4),
\]

\[
b=(1,1,1)^{\mathsf T},
\]

\[
x_0=0.
\]

Then

\[
r_0=b,
\]

\[
Ab=(1,2,4)^{\mathsf T}.
\]

Use the two-column basis

\[
V=[b,Ab]=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&2\\1&4\end{bmatrix}.
\]

The reduced Galerkin system is

\[
(V^{\mathsf T}AV)c=V^{\mathsf T}b.
\]

Direct arithmetic gives

\[
V^{\mathsf T}AV=\begin{bmatrix}7&21\\21&73\end{bmatrix},
\]

\[
V^{\mathsf T}b=\begin{bmatrix}3\\7\end{bmatrix},
\]

and

\[
c=\begin{bmatrix}36/35\\-1/5\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Hence

\[
x_2=\begin{bmatrix}29/35\\22/35\\8/35\end{bmatrix}.
\]

The residual is

\[
r_2=\frac1{35}\begin{bmatrix}6\\-9\\3\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Now check the defining orthogonality:

\[
b^{\mathsf T}r_2=0,
\]

\[
(Ab)^{\mathsf T}r_2=0.
\]

Thus

\[
V^{\mathsf T}r_2=0.
\]

The squared residual norm is

\[
\lVert r_2\rVert_2^2=\frac{18}{175}.
\]

The exact solution is

\[
x_\star=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1/2\\1/4\end{bmatrix}.
\]

The resulting error satisfies

\[
Ae_2=r_2,
\]

as it must.

## 9. The same witness in an orthonormal symmetric basis

Normalize

\[
v_1=\frac1{\sqrt3}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Then

\[
\alpha_1=\frac73,
\]

\[
\beta_1=\frac{\sqrt{14}}3,
\]

and

\[
v_2=\frac1{\sqrt{42}}\begin{bmatrix}-4\\-1\\5\end{bmatrix}.
\]

The next projected diagonal coefficient is

\[
\alpha_2=\frac{59}{21}.
\]

So the two-dimensional projected matrix is

\[
T_2=\begin{bmatrix}7/3&\sqrt{14}/3\\\sqrt{14}/3&59/21\end{bmatrix}.
\]

This is the symmetric tridiagonal reduction in explicit form.

## 10. A conditioning control

Now take

\[
A_c=\operatorname{diag}(1,100,10000),
\]

with the same $b$ and $x_0$.

The one-dimensional SPD Galerkin/conjugate-gradient step has

\[
\alpha=\frac1{3367}.
\]

Its Euclidean residual is

\[
r_1=\frac1{3367}\begin{bmatrix}3366\\3267\\-6633\end{bmatrix},
\]

with

\[
\lVert r_1\rVert_2^2=\frac{19602}{3367}.
\]

That is larger than the initial residual norm squared:

\[
\lVert r_0\rVert_2^2=3.
\]

So a valid conjugate-gradient step can increase Euclidean residual norm.

There is no contradiction.

For SPD systems, the relevant optimality statement for conjugate gradient concerns the A-norm of the error over the affine trial space, not monotone decrease of Euclidean residual norm.

The example therefore separates:

- residual norm;
- error norm;
- energy norm;
- method-specific optimality.

## 11. Matrix-free access

Nothing in the definition requires an explicitly materialized dense matrix.

If the implementation can compute

\[
v\mapsto Av,
\]

it can generate operator-derived directions.

This matters for large sparse systems and for implicit operators.

The real cost then includes:

- operator-vector products;
- orthogonalization;
- projected solves;
- storage;
- preconditioner application;
- restarts.

## 12. Preconditioning changes the problem seen by the iteration

A left-preconditioned system uses

\[
M^{-1}Ax=M^{-1}b.
\]

A right-preconditioned system uses

\[
AM^{-1}y=b,
\]

then recovers

\[
x=M^{-1}y.
\]

The generated space is therefore built from a different effective operator.

Preconditioning is not equivalent to merely taking more iterations.

A good preconditioner changes the geometry or spectrum of the problem in a way that makes the reduced iteration more effective.

## 13. Finite precision

Exact Arnoldi basis vectors are orthogonal.

Finite-precision implementations can lose orthogonality.

For symmetric recurrences, this can produce repeated or distorted spectral information if not controlled.

Possible responses include reorthogonalization, selective reorthogonalization, restarting, or other stabilization strategies.

Each changes cost and information retention.

## 14. Restarting is a tradeoff

Unrestarted Arnoldi stores more basis vectors and pays increasing orthogonalization cost.

Restarting limits memory and work per cycle.

But restarting can also discard useful directions accumulated earlier.

The right tradeoff depends on the operator, spectrum, target accuracy, and memory budget.

## 15. What this chapter does not claim

This chapter does not claim:

- that low-dimensional generated spaces always converge rapidly;
- that small residual always means small solution error;
- that eigenvalues alone determine convergence for non-normal operators;
- that exact-arithmetic orthogonality survives floating point automatically;
- that more iterations are equivalent to better preconditioning;
- that a learned nonlinear architecture inherits classical convergence results merely because it uses iterative transport or operator-generated directions.

## 16. Downstream handoff

Approximate and Structured Attention may inherit the principle that repeated operator action and projection can expose useful low-dimensional structure without full dense computation.

Neural iterative transport may inherit the classical trial-space, residual, projection, and preconditioning vocabulary.

It must independently justify any learned, nonlinear, adaptive, or representation-level extension.
